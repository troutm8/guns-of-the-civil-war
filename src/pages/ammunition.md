The typical infantryman of the Civil War fought with a combination that had existed for barely fifteen years: a rifled barrel, a percussion cap, and a hollow-based bullet that could be loaded as easily as a musket ball.

## The Percussion Cap

The flintlock was replaced in American service only in the 1840s. The **percussion cap** was a small copper cup lined with fulminate of mercury. Placed on a hollow cone (the "nipple") at the breech, it was struck by the hammer and flashed fire into the charge. It was far more reliable than the flint, especially in wet weather.

Muskets and rifle-muskets took the larger **musket cap**, made with four split "wings" so it could be gripped with cold fingers. Revolvers and some carbines used the smaller **pistol cap**. The soldier carried his caps in a **cap pouch** on his belt, lined with sheepskin to keep them from falling out.

## The Minié Ball

The rifle had always been more accurate than the smoothbore musket but slower to load, because its tight-fitting ball had to be forced down the grooves. In 1849 Captain Claude-Étienne Minié of the French army perfected a conical bullet with a **hollow base**, smaller than the bore so that it could be dropped easily down a fouled barrel. When fired, the powder gases expanded the skirt of the base into the rifling.

At the Harpers Ferry Armory, James Henry Burton simplified the design in 1855 so it could be made cheaply and without the iron plug of the French pattern. The American "Minié ball", as the soldiers called it, weighed about 500 grains in .58 caliber. It was carried in a paper cartridge together with about 60 grains of powder.

## The Paper Cartridge

A cartridge was a paper tube holding the powder charge and the bullet. To load a rifle-musket, the soldier:

1. **Tore** the end of the cartridge open with his teeth,
2. **Poured** the powder down the barrel,
3. **Pressed** the bullet into the muzzle and tore away the paper,
4. **Rammed** the bullet home on the powder,
5. **Primed** by placing a cap on the cone, and
6. **Aimed and fired** at the word of command.

The whole of this was set out as a sequence of numbered commands in **Hardee's** and later **Casey's** infantry tactics, which reenactors still drill by today. The soldier carried forty rounds in his cartridge box, and often more in his pockets and knapsack before a battle.

## Combustible and Metallic Cartridges

Breech-loaders needed a different kind of cartridge. The **Sharps** used a combustible linen or nitrated paper cartridge that burned away completely. The **Burnside**, **Smith** and similar carbines used cases of brass, rubber or foil, but were still fired by an external cap.

The future lay with the **self-contained metallic cartridge**, with its priming compound built into the rim of a copper case. The **Spencer** and the **Henry** used rimfire cartridges of this kind. They were far quicker to load and proof against the weather. Their cost, and the North's industrial lead in making them, meant that they remained almost entirely a Federal advantage.

## What the Reenactor Uses

At a reenactment, only **blank cartridges** are used, made to the pattern your unit and event approve. They are torn, poured and primed like the originals, but **never contain a bullet and are never rammed**. Details of blank making, charge size and carriage vary between events, so learn them from your unit's safety officer and not from any book, this one included.
