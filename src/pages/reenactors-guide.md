The arm you carry is the most expensive single item in your kit, and the one your comrades and the public will notice first. It pays to choose carefully, and to choose **after** you have joined a unit, not before.

## Before You Buy

1. **Join a unit first.** Every unit has a standard of authenticity and usually a preferred arm. Many will lend a newcomer a musket for their first few events.
2. **Know your impression.** Which side, which theater, which year, and which branch? A Federal infantryman at Gettysburg and a Confederate cavalryman at Nashville need different arms.
3. **Buy a reproduction made for the hobby.** Reputable makers include Armi Sport (Chiappa), Pedersoli, Pietta and Uberti. Avoid decorator pieces, and never use an original arm on the field. An original is a historical artifact, and a century and a half of age makes its soundness unknowable.
4. **Have it inspected.** Before you fire it at an event, have your unit's ordnance sergeant or safety officer look it over.

## A First Musket

For most new infantry recruits, the choice comes down to two arms:

- **The 1861 Springfield** for Federal impressions, or
- **The three-band 1853 Enfield** for Confederate impressions.

The Enfield is the more flexible choice of the two. It is correct for most Confederate impressions and for many Federal ones, especially in the West. If your unit portrays both sides ("galvanizes"), the Enfield is usually the better buy.

## The Safety Rules

Every event publishes its own safety rules, and they are the rules you follow. The principles behind them are nearly universal:

<div class="caution"><strong>Never load a projectile.</strong> No ball, no bullet, no wadding, no paper that is not part of an approved blank cartridge. Nothing leaves the muzzle but smoke and burning powder.</div>

- **Inspection.** Your arm will be inspected before each battle. Arrive with a clean bore, a clean cone and a working lock.
- **Blank cartridges.** Make them to your unit's pattern, with the charge your event permits, and never ram them. Pour the powder, discard the paper, and prime.
- **Rammers stay in their channels** during battle scenarios.
- **Elevate when firing.** Aim over the heads of the opposing line, at the angle your event requires, and never fire at an opponent within the minimum distance set by the rules.
- **Misfires.** If your arm fails to fire, keep it pointed safely upward, re-cap and try again. If it still fails, do not reload on top of the unfired charge. Fall out and see the ordnance sergeant.
- **Bayonets** are fixed only on command and are never used against a person.
- **Hand-to-hand** fighting, clubbing with a musket, or anything that brings your muzzle near another person is forbidden at nearly every event.
- **Revolvers and cartridge arms** have rules of their own. Know them before you take the field.

## Care of the Arm

Black powder fouling attracts moisture and rusts steel quickly. Clean your arm **the same day** you fire it: hot, soapy water through the bore, then dry thoroughly and oil. Clean the cone and the lock. Keep a nipple wrench, a spare cone, a worm, a wiper and cleaning patches in your kit. They were all period items, and a soldier who knows how to use them makes a good impression on the public.
