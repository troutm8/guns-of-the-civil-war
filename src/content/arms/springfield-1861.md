The Model 1861 was the arm that the Union Army carried to victory. It descended directly from the Model 1855 rifle-musket, which had introduced the .58-caliber bore and the Minié system to American service. The new pattern dropped the 1855's costly and troublesome Maynard tape primer, which fed paper primers onto the cone like a toy cap-gun, and returned to the plain percussion lock and musket cap. The change made the arm quicker and cheaper to build at the moment when speed and cheapness mattered above all else.

## Development

Springfield Armory was the only national armory left to the Union after Virginia militia burned the Harpers Ferry works in April 1861. Springfield enlarged its workforce and ran two shifts, but it could not meet the demand alone. The War Department therefore let contracts to private makers, among them Colt, Lamson, Goodrich & Yale, Providence Tool Company and Amoskeag Manufacturing. Most contract arms follow the Springfield pattern closely enough that their parts will interchange, though Colt's so-called "Special Model" departs from it in several respects.

The arm is a three-band rifle-musket: long enough to serve as a pike with the bayonet fixed, and rifled so that it could strike a man-sized target at several hundred yards in the hands of a trained soldier. The sights were simple: a fixed block for 100 yards and two folding leaves for 300 and 500 yards.

## In the Field

By 1863 the Springfield was the most common arm in the Army of the Potomac, and it gradually displaced the imported Enfields and Austrians in the Western armies as well. Confederate troops picked up thousands on the battlefields of 1862 and valued them highly.

Recent scholarship has questioned how much the rifle's long range changed combat. Most infantry engagements were still fought at a hundred yards or less, partly for want of marksmanship training and partly because of the Minié ball's steeply curving flight, which demanded careful judging of distance. The rifle-musket's reputation for making war more deadly may owe more to the size of the armies and the length of the campaigns than to the arm itself.

## Variants

The **Model 1863 Type I** simplified the lock plate, band springs and hammer. The **Type II**, sometimes wrongly called the "Model 1864", returned to band springs and used a single-leaf rear sight. See the separate article on the [Model 1863](../springfield-1863/).

## For the Reenactor

For a Federal infantry impression from 1862 on, few arms are more correct than the 1861 Springfield. Reproductions are plentiful and parts are easy to find. Units generally expect:

- **Lock markings** that match your impression's date. A lock dated 1863 is out of place at a First Bull Run event.
- **A serviceable bayonet and scabbard**, even where bayonets are not fixed in action.
- **A spark shield** (a hammer shroud to protect the men beside you) only where your unit or event asks for one. It is a modern safety item, not a period one.
- **A tight-fitting cone (nipple)** and a musket-cap sized to it. Four-wing "top hat" musket caps are the period form.

Keep the bore clean between firings and after every event. Black-powder fouling is corrosive, and a pitted bore or a worn cone is the first thing a safety inspector will see.
