The Pattern 1853 Enfield was adopted by the British Army in 1853 and first saw action in the Crimea. By 1861 it was the best-known rifle-musket in the world, and the gunmakers of Birmingham and London could turn it out faster than any American factory could make Springfields. Both governments sent agents to England within months of Fort Sumter. The Confederacy's Major Caleb Huse and the Union's purchasing agents often bid against each other for the same stocks.

## Development

The Enfield's .577 bore was close enough to the American .58 that the two arms could, in practice, fire each other's cartridges. That made it an easy fit with Federal supply. The rear sight was more ambitious than the Springfield's: a ladder sight graduated to 900 yards, reflecting the British Army's more thorough training in musketry.

Many "Enfields" in American service were not made at the Royal Small Arms Factory at all. They came from the Birmingham and London trade, often assembled from parts by many small shops. They were frequently marked with a crown over "Tower" and a date, and in Confederate service sometimes with a JS and anchor inspection stamp.

## In the Field

Blockade runners brought Enfields through Wilmington and Charleston in great quantity, and by 1863 the Enfield was the most characteristic arm of the Confederate infantryman. The Union bought large numbers too, particularly in 1861–62, and they were common in Western regiments until Springfields became plentiful.

Soldiers generally rated the Enfield highly, second only to the Springfield. The best of them, especially the London-made arms and later Enfield-made pieces with interchangeable parts, were thought equal or superior to the American arm.

## Variants

The **Pattern 1858 naval rifle** and the **Pattern 1856 and 1860 short rifles** were two-band arms with a 33-inch barrel, often fitted with a sword bayonet. Both saw American service in smaller numbers, especially with Confederate troops.

## For the Reenactor

The three-band Enfield is the safest single purchase for a **Confederate infantry** impression, and it remains correct for many Federal Western impressions through 1863. When buying:

- Look for a reproduction with **Tower** lock markings and a period date (1861–1863 is most useful).
- The Enfield takes a **musket cap**, the same as the Springfield.
- Keep the **brass furniture** dull. Soldiers did not polish it to a shine in the field.
- Choose a period-correct **cartridge box**. Confederate soldiers often used imported British boxes or Southern-made copies of the U.S. pattern.
