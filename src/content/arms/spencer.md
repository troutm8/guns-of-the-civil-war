Christopher Spencer was a young engineer at Cheney Brothers silk mills when he patented his repeating rifle in 1860. The design was simple and rugged: a tube of seven copper rimfire cartridges was inserted through the butt plate, and a lever beneath the breech fed them one at a time. The soldier worked the lever, cocked the hammer by hand, and fired.

## Development

The Navy placed the first government order, and the Army followed with rifles and, from 1863, carbines. Spencer is said to have demonstrated the rifle personally to President Lincoln on the White House lawn in August 1863, and Lincoln's approval helped speed its adoption. The **Blakeslee cartridge box**, a leather case holding six to thirteen loaded tubes, let the soldier reload the magazine in seconds.

## In the Field

Spencer rifles first saw action in 1863. At Hoover's Gap in June 1863, Colonel John T. Wilder's mounted infantry brigade, which had bought its own Spencers on credit, held off a much larger Confederate force. The Confederates are said to have thought they faced a division. By 1864 Spencer carbines were the standard arm of much of the Federal cavalry. At Franklin and Nashville and on Wilson's Raid of 1865, their firepower was decisive.

The Spencer's great limitation was its ammunition. Rimfire cartridges were expensive and hard to supply, and some officers worried soldiers would waste them. The Confederacy could not make the cartridges, so the captured Spencers it acquired were of limited use.

## For the Reenactor

A Spencer is correct for **late-war Federal cavalry** and for the handful of infantry units that carried it. Most reproductions are chambered for modern centerfire cartridges. Ask your unit what blank system, if any, it approves. Many units carry the Spencer unfired, or use blank cartridges from an approved supplier. **Never improvise ammunition for a cartridge arm.**
