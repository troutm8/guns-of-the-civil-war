Sir Joseph Whitworth, the great Manchester engineer, was asked by the British government in 1854 to improve the Enfield. His answer was a rifle with a small **hexagonal bore** and a very fast twist, firing a long bullet shaped to fit the hexagon. In trials it far outshot the Enfield. It was too costly and too easily fouled for general service, but it was taken up eagerly by target shooters and, in small numbers, by the Confederacy.

## In the Field

The Confederate government bought Whitworth rifles in England and ran them through the blockade, a few at a time. They were issued to the best shots in picked sharpshooter units, most famously in **Cleburne's Division** of the Army of Tennessee. Some were fitted with Davidson telescopic sights mounted on the left side of the stock.

Whitworth marksmen are credited with long-range hits on Federal officers and artillerymen. The most famous of these, the death of Major General John Sedgwick at Spotsylvania in May 1864, is often attributed to a Whitworth, though the claim cannot be proved.

## For the Reenactor

The Whitworth is correct only for a **Confederate sharpshooter** impression, and it should be carried by someone who can explain it. Reproductions are expensive. Most live-fire competitors use them in North-South Skirmish Association matches rather than in battle reenactments. On the reenactment field the arm is fired only with blanks, like any other.
