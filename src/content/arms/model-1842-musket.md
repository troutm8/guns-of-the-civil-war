The Model 1842 was the first U.S. musket made on the percussion system from the start and the first made at both national armories with fully interchangeable parts. It was a handsome, sturdy arm, and many men who carried it in 1861 thought it a better weapon at close range than the new rifles.

## Development

The Model 1842 was designed at the close of the flintlock era, and its lock was the first standard percussion lock in American service. A great many were later **rifled and sighted** at the armories in 1855–1859, following the adoption of the Minié ball. These altered arms are usually called "rifled Model 1842s", and they served widely in the war's first two years.

## In the Field

Smoothbores were considered obsolete by 1861, but they were what the arsenals held. Many regiments on both sides went to war with them. At the short ranges where most fighting took place, the **buck and ball** load was devastating: a .69 round ball backed by three buckshot. Some commanders, notably in the Irish Brigade, preferred it and resisted exchanging their smoothbores for rifles.

## For the Reenactor

A smoothbore Model 1842 is correct for many **1861–62 impressions** on both sides. It remained common in Confederate service longer than in Union service. It is a good choice for units that portray early-war volunteers, state troops or militia. Check that your unit accepts the .69 bore. The larger bore takes a larger blank charge, and some events limit charge sizes.
